Kitchen Knife Guide: What to Buy First and What the Steel Number Means

Updated July 26, 2026 · WhatAreBest.com Editorial Team

Most people own too many knives and the wrong ones. Here is what to actually buy and why the steel hardness rating matters in practice.

The Only Three Knives You Actually Need

The vast majority of kitchen cutting tasks can be handled by: an 8-inch chef's knife (everything), a 4-inch paring knife (detail work), and a 10-12 inch serrated bread knife (bread, tomatoes, anything with a tough skin). A complete 10-piece knife block set typically includes 7 knives you will rarely touch. Buy three great knives instead.

Rockwell Hardness (HRC) in Practice

52-56 HRC (most Western knives -- Wusthof, Henckels): easier to sharpen on any whetstone, more flexible, less likely to chip on hard foods. 58-62 HRC (most Japanese knives -- Shun, MAC, Global): takes a sharper edge and holds it longer, but requires a finer-grit whetstone and chips more easily on hard foods or bones. For general home cooking, German/Western HRC 56 is more practical. For vegetable-forward cooking, Japanese HRC 60+ is outstanding.

Handle Ergonomics Matter

A knife you never want to put down has a handle shaped correctly for your grip. Western bolster knives are most stable for pinch-grip users. Japanese wa handles are lighter but require practice for comfortable extended use. Hold the knife before buying if possible -- or buy from a retailer with easy returns.

Honing vs. Sharpening

These are not the same. Honing (steel rod) realigns the edge microstructure -- it does not remove metal. Sharpening removes metal to create a new edge. Hone before every use; sharpen every 3-6 months. A honed knife used consistently performs better than a sharpened knife that is never honed.
